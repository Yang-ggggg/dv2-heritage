Who Decides What Goes Online?

Law, money and demand behind Australia's digital past

Every day, libraries, archives and museums put more of Australia's past online. Not everything can go online at once. Which records go first? This page follows three forces through the data.

  1. Law: copyright rules shape which years go online.
  2. Money: funding helps decide which states grow.
  3. Demand: popular records are often digitised first.

Main colours show the source: blue National Library, amber National Archives, green National Museum. Grey means other or not online.

Section 1 · Growth

How much is online?

Trove is the National Library's free search website. It includes digitised old newspapers. In 2011 it held 48.5 million articles. By October 2026 it held 266.3 million, 5.5 times as many.

Most of the growth happened before 2017. From 2017 to 2025, Trove added about 5 million articles a year.

Then it added 15.3 million articles in under two years. Queensland supplied 10.5 million of them.

The National Archives of Australia also digitises old files, such as war service records.

It worked fastest in 2022. Since then, it has slowed down every year.

11,286

files a week on average in 2022 (recorded weeks), compared with 2,025 in 2026

Section 2 · Law

The copyright wall

To manage copyright, the library mostly digitises papers from before 1955. Later papers need a deal with the publisher.

Trove itself says its newspapers are "generally" from before 1956. Since February 2025, Trove added 4.9 million articles from 1955 or later.

79% fewer

articles from 1955 than from 1954

Newspapers are only one kind of collection. The National Library also keeps manuscripts: letters, diaries and private papers. Its finding aids list the items inside each collection.

A collection's start year is the earliest year it covers. Early collections have the most items online. Collections starting from the 1930s to the 1980s have only 1% to 5.5% online.

97% or more

of items in collections that begin in the 1760s and 1770s are online

Note: squares are rounded, so 29.5% shows as 30 squares.

Note: decades with fewer than 20 collections are left out. A few collections could swing their share.

Do all collections stop at the same year? No. Newspapers fall sharply after the 1950s.

Periodicals, manuscripts and oral histories reach much later. Most oral histories were recorded from the 1970s to the 2010s.

Section 3 · Money

Where you live matters

Digitising costs money. The Trove Data Guide says New South Wales "invested large amounts of money" through its Digital Excellence program. Its newspapers grew 9.3 times since 2011. The Northern Territory grew only 1.2 times.

Rows are states and territories. Hollow dots show April 2011. Filled dots show October 2026. The scale grows by multiples, so a longer line means the state grew more times over.

Totals and per resident counts tell different stories. New South Wales has the most articles. But per resident, Tasmania has the most.

Per resident means all digitised articles divided by the people who lived there in March 2026. Both maps use the same map scale.

Section 4 · Demand

Popular records often go first

The National Archives of Australia lists the files it digitises each week. Its recorded lists hold 1.49 million files, March 2021 to September 2026.

More than half are Second World War service records, one of its most requested kinds of record. Migration and citizenship records come next.

56%

of recorded archive files are Second World War service records

The mix changes from month to month. In mid-2022, migration records took over for a few months.

Then service records came back. From July to October 2024, they fell to under 10% of files.

72%

of files digitised in June 2022 were migration records

Note: some weekly lists are missing in January 2022, January 2023 and December 2023. Those months use the recorded weeks. No lists exist from May 2025 to May 2026, so the chart stops in April 2025.

Section 5 · People

Voices and objects

The National Library lists 20,083 oral history recordings. Only 6,202 have audio online. Only 1,781 offer a transcript to download. Of the audio-only recordings, 2,710 offer a summary to download.

People help where they can. Trove users fix mistakes in the computer-read text of old newspapers.

17.5 million

newspaper articles with text corrected by Trove users (October 2026)

On 4 October 2026, the National Museum's online catalogue gave 97,214 object records. 18,962 had a production place with map coordinates. After the exclusions below, the map shows 14,348: 14.8% of those records. Many were produced around Sydney, Melbourne and Canberra. A large group was produced around Yirrkala in Arnhem Land.

How to read

Each hexagon covers the same geographic area. The map uses the first production place with map coordinates in each record. Darker green means more objects. Empty land has no mapped objects.

What is left out

3,168 objects with an overseas production place. 1,435 objects placed only in a state or wide region, for example "Central Australia". 11 objects outside the map.

Why this hexagon size

Each hexagon is 100 kilometres from centre to corner. With 150-kilometre hexagons, the same four areas stay on top.

What Malaysia can learn

Three lessons for Malaysian libraries, archives and museums.

Start with what copyright allows. Old works that are out of copyright can go online first.

Measure regional gaps first. Trove's state counts show uneven growth. Check gaps like this before deciding where to spend.

Ask the public to help. Trove users corrected the text of 17.5 million articles. Volunteers could help Malaysian collections too.

How the data was prepared

Every chart is drawn with Vega-Lite 5, except the treemap, which uses Vega 5. Python (pandas) was used only to clean, join and count the data. The hexagons were counted in Python and drawn by Vega-Lite. Map boundaries were simplified with mapshaper.

Trove counts were updated on 20 October 2026 with my own Trove API key, using the same search settings as Tim Sherratt's harvester. The key is not stored on GitHub.

Missing weeks and years are shown as missing, never as zero. Each script in the pipeline folder on GitHub prints the rows it drops.

Some numbers are worked out from others. Articles per resident is all digitised articles divided by residents in March 2026. Share digitised is digitised items divided by all items. Files per week means monthly files divided by recorded weeks. Transcripts and summaries are the oral history fields "transcript" and "summary" (available to download).

Archive themes come from words in each file's title, such as "Service Number". Series titles in the tooltips are lightly edited to spell out short forms.

All maps use an Albers equal-area conic projection. Areas keep their true size, so they can be compared. Shapes bend a little near the edges. Map classes use round numbers.

Code references

Charts are loaded with vega-embed, as in the FIT3179 multiple charts example. The two state maps follow the FIT3179 symbol map and choropleth examples (TopoJSON shapes and a lookup join). The treemap uses the Vega treemap transform, as in the Vega treemap example. The streamgraph uses the centred stack from the Vega-Lite streamgraph example. All specs were written for this page.
