Who Decides What Goes Online?

Law, money and demand behind Australia's digital past

Every day, libraries, archives and museums put more of Australia's past online. But they cannot put everything online at once. Something has to decide what goes first and what waits. This page follows three forces through the data.

  1. Law: copyright decides which years can go online.
  2. Money: funding decides which states grow.
  3. Demand: what people ask for is digitised first.

Section 1

How much is online?

Trove is the National Library's free website for old newspapers. In 2011 it held 48.5 million articles. By February 2025 it held 251.0 million, about five times more.

Most of the growth happened before 2017. Since then, Trove has added about 5 million articles a year.

Section 2 · Law

The copyright wall

Old newspapers are out of copyright, so the library can put them online. Newer papers are still protected. Trove says its newspapers are "generally" from before 1956, and later papers appear only when the publisher gives permission.

88% fewer

articles from 1955 than from 1954

Newspapers are only one kind of collection. The National Library also keeps manuscripts: letters, diaries and private papers. Only 3 in 10 manuscript items are online. The oldest papers come first.

Section 3 · Money

Where you live matters

Digitising costs money, and each state library decides how much to spend. New South Wales funded a large program, so its newspapers have grown nine times since 2011. The Northern Territory barely grew. Per resident, Tasmania now has the most.

Section 4 · Demand

What people ask for goes first

The National Archives of Australia digitised 1.49 million files from March 2021 to September 2026.

More than half are Second World War service records, one of its most requested kinds of record. Migration and citizenship records come next.

56%

of digitised archive files are Second World War service records

The mix changes from month to month. In mid-2022, migration records took over for a few months. Then service records came back.

72%

Note: January 2022, January 2023 and December 2023 miss some weekly lists. They show the average of the weeks recorded. Later months are not shown. Weekly lists are missing from May 2025 to May 2026.

Section 5 · People

Voices and objects

The National Library holds 20,083 oral history recordings. Only 6,202 can be heard online. Only 1,781 have a written transcript, so most voices cannot be searched as text.

People help where they can. Trove users have corrected the text of 16.3 million newspaper articles.

16.3 million

newspaper articles with text corrected by Trove users (February 2025)

The National Museum of Australia also shares its objects online. For 18,962 of its 97,214 objects, the museum records where they were made. Many come from Sydney, Melbourne and Canberra. But one in three mapped objects was made in the Northern Territory.

34%

of mapped objects come from the Northern Territory, home to 1% of Australians (March 2026)

How to read

Each hexagon covers the same area of land. Darker green means more objects were made there. Empty land has no mapped objects.

What is left out

3,168 objects made overseas. 1,435 objects that only name a state or a large region, for example "Central Australia". 11 objects placed off the map.

Why this hexagon size

Each hexagon is 100 kilometres from centre to corner. With 150-kilometre hexagons, the same four areas stay on top.

What Malaysia can learn

These lessons may help Malaysian libraries, archives and museums plan what to digitise first.

Start with what copyright allows. Old works that are out of copyright can go online first.

Share the money fairly. Without shared funding, some regions fall behind, like the Northern Territory.

Ask the public to help. Trove users corrected the text of 16.3 million articles. Volunteers could help Malaysian collections too.

How the data was prepared

Every chart is drawn with Vega-Lite 5, except the treemap, which uses Vega 5. Python (pandas) was used only to clean, join and count the data. The hexagons were counted in Python and drawn by Vega-Lite. Map boundaries were simplified with mapshaper.

Missing weeks and years are shown as missing, never as zero. Each script in the pipeline folder on GitHub prints the rows it drops.

Some numbers are worked out from others. Articles per resident is all digitised articles divided by residents in March 2026. Share digitised is digitised items divided by all items. Files per week means monthly files divided by recorded weeks.

Archive themes come from words in each file's title, such as "Service Number". Series titles in the tooltips are lightly edited to spell out short forms.

All maps use an Albers equal-area conic projection, so areas can be compared fairly. Map classes use round numbers.
